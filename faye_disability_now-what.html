Now What?



This exploration of special issues on accessibility and disability in TPC publications showed that the field started on shaky ground, as the authors of the 1985 IEEE special issue articles defined people with disabilities as “disadvantaged readers.” In 1985, accessibility meant that benevolent TPCers did extra work to help a small group of people who could not participate in society due to their impairment. In the TC 2006 special issue, the focus was on making technology accessible, but the perspective on disability and the ADA was mostly uncritical and apolitical. Jumping to 2018, we had a completely different stance: Disability is recognized as an important part of life that is often ignored by the TPC field. Believing that knowledge can bring change, the authors in the two BPCQ issues wanted their readers to learn about disability studies theory, increase their interest in the topic, and bring it into their classes. The authors in the CDQ’s special issue went even further, digging deep into the complexity of bridging disability studies with TPC. Finally, in IEEE’s 2021 issue, the authors called for TPCers to specialize in disability studies, merge pedagogy on accessibility with social justice, and self-scrutinize to stop our collaboration with an ableist world.

I would be a lot more excited by this increased commitment to the topic of disability if I could observe it spreading to the whole field. However, as noted by Heidi Willers’s (2024) study published in IEEE, the field is still not embracing the paradigm shift of including disabled users in our practices and research.

Why are the repeated calls on changing our relationship with disability left unanswered?

There are more answers to this question than I could fit into this web-text, so here I will focus on the three possibilities that have haunted me the most throughout my exploration and analysis.

Possibility 1: We are just performing a disciplinary interest.

Rather than demonstrating a true disciplinary commitment within TPC, the special issues might be performing a disciplinary interest in the topic of disability. Sara Ahmed’s (2012) analysis on the performance of institutional called for diversity when in practice universities remain inhospitable to all the people who do not fit racist, sexist, heteronormative, and ableist standards highlights a potential similarity in the work of the special issues. Just like performing an interest in diversity does not lead to actual change, the special issues could be reassuring the field that we are doing good work without translating that performance into action. Of course, the editors and authors of the special issues were genuinely trying to change things, but the larger field might embrace the special issue as a “good enough” commitment to the topic without further investigating if any of this is implemented in how we do things in TPC. After all, as various TPC scholars have pointed out, our scholarship does not necessarily materialize into real change just because it focuses on social justice (Natasha Jones et al., 2014; Josephine Walwema, 2021).

The repetition of the same calls in the special issues over 35+ years illustrates what happens when we stop our work at a performance of interest: Nothing progresses. At the individual level, TPCers who merge TPC and disability studies are having important conversations in recent publications, such as focusing on disability rights, examining ableist practices within academia, and seeking ways to bring a critical perspective to our accessibility work (Ann Amicucci, 2024; Kristin Bennett, 2023, 2024; S. Cavar & ulysses/constance bougie, 2024; Phillip Gallagher & Marci Gallagher, 2024; Leah Heilig et al., 2024). But these scholars are few, and their inside conversations are not spreading in a way that would significantly change how we do things in the field. By just performing an interest, we are left with a superficial connection to the topic, which means that many of our practices remain locked on checklists and retrofit accommodations, and that we are not self-scrutinizing enough to uncover how TPC might be contributing to an ableist system.

Possibility 2: We think of disability as a useless topic.

Another possibility for why we are not answering the calls from the scholars bridging disability studies and TPC might be that in our search for disciplinary power and legitimacy, we are not interested in a topic that is deemed useless by academia (Ahmed, 2019). Academic ableism systematically devalues people with disabilities, creating roadblocks for disabled scholars, perpetuating the idea that disabilities have no place in the university, and relegating the topic of disability to the margins of research projects (Jay Dolmage, 2008, 2017; Ada Hubrig, 2023; Sephanie Kerschbaum, 2014; Simi Linton, 1998; Margaret Price, 2011).

As Ahmed (2019) explained, universities are governed by a neoliberal utilitarianism that rewards researchers who study topics that will bring money and glory to the university. This administrative hierarchy of topics is problematic for any discipline, but even more so for TPC and its struggle for institutional power and recognition. The field TPC has morphed from service courses to departments with doctoral programs, but its scholarship shows unease over its lowly place within academia, often debating its status at length: being too academic or too vocational (Louise Rheling, 2004); too detached from industry (Marjorie Davis, 2004); or overly or insufficiently humanistic (Carolyn Miller, 1979). Indeed, Jones et al. (2014) argue that this focus over the field’s status has delayed attention to other issues such as diversity in TPC programs. Even worse, Teresa Kynell-Hunt (2003) argues that by looking at both academia and industry to confer us our status, we have reinforced the power structures that look down on us. Disability, depicted in negative terms by academic ableism, might not be a popular topic with scholars who seek more academic prestige for our discipline.

While seemingly a low-prestige topic, research on disability could be transformative for TPC: It would help us reveal lingering disciplinary ableism and prompt us to action towards social change. However, if we truly want to bring TPC and disability together as a social justice project in our field, we need to make a pledge larger than just calling for accessibility. As the authors of the IEEE 2021 issue demonstrated, we must first self-scrutinize deeply before we can do true anti-ableist work. Then, maybe the answer to the question “How has the topic of disability evolved within TPC?” will cease to be “a lot for a few TPC disability experts, not so much for the whole field.”

Possibility 3: We still love a checklist.

Even though the experts bridging disability and TPC emphatically warn TPCers that the field’s tendency to think of accessibility work as simply following a checklist reinforces ableist ideologies (Sushil Oswal & Lisa Melonçon, 2017; Sean Zdenek, 2020), this tendency lingers on and still needs to be called out and argued against (Joanna Schreiber, 2025). This narrow idea of accessibility work is a pity because it does not match the profound theoretical shifts that have happened within the field in terms of pedagogy as a tool for social justice (Sonka, Kate, et al., 2021; Bennett, Kristin C. & Hannah, Mark. A., 2021) and of TPC’s complex understanding of disabled users’ relationship to technology (Baker et al., 2021; Zdenek 2009; 2018b). The present situation is a case of disciplinary ideas moving faster than the field’s practices, and of the difficulty of moving off the beaten path of doing things—even though many individual TPCers care deeply about disabled users.

Recommendations

Interpreting disability as a gain for TPC scholarship would, at the very least,

  • center difference in how we picture our users and remove the illusion that some people fit norms while others do not (Sean Zdenek, 2018b),
  • highlight that we are “all technologically embodied” and stop us from segregating people who use adaptive technology (Lisa Melonçon, 2013, p. 79), and
  • help us notice ableist tendencies in our field.

Thus, disability studies theory can strengthen our theoretical and social justice work in TPC, moving us from thinking of certain groups as problematic to focusing on the power structures that create injustice (Adrienne Asch, 2005; Kristin Bennett & Mark Hannah, 2022; Lennard Davis, 1995).

Based on my findings, I offer some recommendations to the TPC field. While numbered, they are not in any prioritized order. Rather, they work together to bring us to a more socially just field.

1. Expand the conversations on disability so that the topic is not relegated to special issues.

Many of the articles that focus on disability (in and out of special issues) contain urgent calls for TPC scholars to do more research on this topic (to name just a few: Geoffrey Clegg, 2018; Heilig, 2023; Sherena Huntsman, 2021; Melinda Knight, 2018; Melonçon, 2013; Sushil Oswal, 2013; Oswal & Melonçon, 2014). These calls are repeated because the field is responding to them lackadaisically. Our first effort, then, should be to normalize this topic for the field by publishing articles on disability more regularly. Indeed, some journals trail behind in publishing articles on disability outside of special issues. There are others, though, that are useful models for this recommendation. CDQ publishes regularly on disability, without waiting for a special issue to frame the conversation. Similarly, Kairos’s recurrent publishing on this topic is a commendable example for the rest of the field.

One way to spark that interest in this topic and thus improve its presence in TPC publications might be following up on the calls made by the authors of the BPCQ’s 81.1 special issue: Bring the topic to TPC courses, not as a one-time activity, but as a complex conversation with disability studies scholars (Clegg, 2018; Heilig, 2018; Hitt, 2018; Melonçon, 2018; Nielsen, 2018; Palmer & Palmer, 2018; Wheeler, 2018; Youngblood & Brooks, 2018). Sparking an early interest in disability justice in TPC students could transform TPC research trends in the future.

2. Answer (at last!) the call for the paradigm shift.

Of all the insights I gained from this research project, none has been more surprising than the field’s stubbornness in ignoring the repeated calls to center disability in our research and practices to avoid retrofit accommodations and to include disabled users at every step of our projects (to name just a few, as this is the most repeated call in the articles on disability: Mary Clinkenbeard 2020; Jodie Gilmore, 1989; Oswal, 2014; Mary Frances Theofanos & Janice (Ginny) Redish, 2005; Willers, 2024). TPC has not yet shifted to fully and consistently valuing the insights from disabled users, and this gap in knowledge, when coupled with our tendency to think of users as nondisabled, becomes particularly damaging. What is more, not including disabled users in our conversations on technology leads some scholars into an acute focus on technology, which can lead to “technoableism”—the idea that technology can fix all problems related to disability (Lichen Hamraie, 2023, p. 305). This happens when we think of technology as the panacea for people with disabilities, without realizing that focusing on technology rather than people makes people with disabilities dependent on the technology and further isolated from the rest of the population (Kimberly Elmore, 2013). With the overtaking of AI in all things technological, we now must commit even further to checking our technoableist tendencies and to foster real conversations with disabled users.

3. Embrace the idea that focusing on disability leads us to do better TPC work.

In his proposal to make disability a topic of research in TPC, Oswal (2013) listed the professional opportunities that would open for TPCers, such as being translators of legal mandates and guidelines on accessibility, designers with accessibility expertise, and researchers filling a vast research gap in industries trying to gain more customers. As Oswal described, our expertise in accessibility could add marketability, relevance, and clout to TPCers in the workplace, thus giving us an important seat at the table. But besides professional opportunities, focusing on disability also helps us be better social justice activists as we embrace projects that are explicitly anti-ableist. Zdenek (2018b), through a discussion on enhanced and creative captions that do more than just transcribe speech, illustrates how accessibility practices that go beyond the mere minimum can have a radical effect on our society by challenging its norms and complacency on how things are done. Additionally, Elmore (2013) argued that bringing in disabled users as equal partners in our user-centered projects would “liberate both [disabled users and the project’s designers] from the limitations of their necessarily circumscribed perspectives” and engage them in useful conversations where the sum of the different knowledges is more than its parts (p. 33). Shifting our disciplinary knowledge to include accessibility for social change would make us cutting-edge leaders in accessibility work, participating in disability studies conversations rather than just following them from afar—always a bit behind.

4. Explore all disabilities.

Some disabilities are completely ignored by our journals, while others are so recurring as to become stereotypes on how we think of disabled users. My review of the publication history in TPC journals shows most articles focusing on hearing, visual, and mobility impairments. Then, there are a few articles researching cognitive and learning disabilities; among those articles only one focuses on Alzheimer’s disease (Marsha Durham, 1999), one on schizophrenia (Theo Manschreck, 1986), and one on dyslexia (Christin Phelps, 2021). There are no articles on intellectual disabilities. Bringing ignored types of disabilities into the conversation might help TPCers understand the complexities of this topic and encourage them to explore other groups within the large disability community.

A first improvement toward that goal could be to publish the first TPC article on intellectual disabilities in our main publications. My academic institution has a program, the Redwood SEED Scholars Program, designed to welcome people with intellectual disabilities into college classes to teach them life skills and independent living. I meet some of the programs’ students in my classes and am always wondering how I could improve their experience in my courses. How should I revise my instruction to meet their needs? As a TPCer, what kind of support could I give them as they navigate academia? How can I create useful resources for when they enter the workforce and live independently? For now, I will not find these answers within TPC major publications.

A personal call to embrace disability as a crucial topic in TPC

In a world bent on removing social programs and protections for marginalized populations, it is even more urgent for TPC to continue paying attention to disability rights, all marginalized users, and opportunities to effectively safeguard access to information and resources. My interest in TPC lies in its double nature: being both the voice of the system and the voice of resistance (Cecilia Shelton & Sarah Warren-Riley, 2022). I end by calling for an even stronger commitment to being the voice of resistance and a haven for those shipwrecked by unjust turns of the tide. This would cement TPC’s reputation as a contrarian, activist, maybe slightly illegal field—but one that I would be proud to call my own.
