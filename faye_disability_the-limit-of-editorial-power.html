The Limits of Editorial Power

The readership of BPCQ did not seem particularly interested in continuing the conversation on disability after the 2018 special issues, publishing only four disability-focused articles after the special issues (Peter Cardon, 2025; Sherrie Drye et al., 2023; Lorelei Ortiz, 2020; Alessandra Rossetti & Luuk Van Waes, 2022). The topic does not completely drop off the map after the journal’s special issues, though. Melinda Knight, BPCQ editor from 2010 to 2022, kept attention on the topic through a series of editorials.

The 2019 editorial, “Communicating Obsolescence: When Assistive Technology is Canceled or Discontinued,” described Knight’s personal experience using speech-to-text dictation and her frustration when the program inexplicably disappears. Knight called for more research on unexplored issues relating to disabilities, such as discontinued assistive technology leaving people depending on it without alternatives. She also pointed out that none of the articles in the current BPCQ issue discussed accessibility, wondering if our field’s research trends had been tainted by ableism.

Knight’s 2020 editorial “Supporting Neurodiverse Students in Business and Professional Communication Classes: Opportunities and Challenges” focused on neurodiverse students. It highlighted at length the only article about disability in that issue, which focused on neurodiverse students, praising it for continuing the work started in the 2018 special issues on disability.

In the 2021 editorial, “Promoting Accessibility in the Zoom Era,” Knight again brought up her personal experience. Being hard of hearing, assistive technology had been an important tool for her before and during the pandemic. Pointing out that “people with disabilities have been especially affected” by the pandemic (p. 291), Knight called on teachers to make sure we prioritize accessibility during the pandemic and cited an article by Sushil Oswal and Zsuzsanna Palmer (2021) published in another journal, hoping to see similar work in BPCQ. However, except for this editorial, there are no articles about disability in this issue.

Editorial decisions might not be the only reason why disability as a topic does not show up more often in TPC journals. Knight’s (2019, 2020, 2021) editorials called on researchers to address disability, going so far as to make explicit accusations of ableism in the field. Knight’s frustration on being ignored comes through in these editorials, and it is even more shocking when we think that it is the main editor of the journal who is being thus ignored. Knight’s scolding the authors in BPCQ for being skewed by ableism and her reprimand on the whole field for ignoring the issues of disabled users were strong condemnations of TPC—and yet they appeared muted by the lack of response. Thus, Knight’s editorials illustrated Sara Ahmed’s (2012) argument that TPC might feel satisfied with the performance of naming disability without actually doing the work and self-reflecting on how its own ableism might lead it to ignore the topic.

We are reminded here that despite having individual disability “champions” (Ahmed, 2012) like Knight, anti-ableism must be a team effort within the whole field. This effort, though, is blocked by hidden disciplinary ableism within both TPC and academia. In fact, we do not have to search far to recognize the glaring sexist and ableist ideologies within academia: The embodied experience of Melinda Knight, a woman with disabilities who, despite her position of power as editor of a major publication, finds her calls for social just accessibility unheeded.
