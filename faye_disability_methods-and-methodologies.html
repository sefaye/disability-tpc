Methods and Methodologies

The general research question that guided me during this project was: How has TPC journal coverage of disability changed over time, given the passionate calls for change and the limited responses to those calls?

To answer it, I examined the entire publication history of the main journals in TPC scholarship.

Data Collection

I focused on journals because I wanted to learn more deeply about the field of TPC, and journals are “a clear reflection of [a] discipline’s past, a synchronic portrait of its current state, and a glimpse of its dreams and plans for the future” (Robert Connors, 1984, p. 348). Connors also warns that they shape the field “as loci of disciplinary authority, as editorial soapboxes or coxswain’s benches, as steppingstones and milestones” (p. 348). Indeed, reviewing the journals in the field of composition, Connors argues that journals are where the ideas of a field are both shared and filtered, molding the trends in the field and its body of knowledge. Far from being neutral endeavors, journals highlight the political aspects governing which topics thrive and which are ignored (Maureen Goggin, 2000).

While I searched for all articles focusing on disability and accessibility in selected TPC publications, the core of my analysis rests on the special issues on this topic. I think of them as time capsules of particular moments when the field made space for conversations on disability.

I started my dataset by collecting all the articles and introductions from six special issues published in four TPC journals:

  • IEEE Transactions on Professional Communication (IEEE), 1985 (28.4) and 2021 (64.3)
  • Technical Communication (TC), 2006 (53.1)
  • Business and Professional Communication Quarterly (BPCQ), 2018 (81.1) and (81.4)
  • Communication Design Quarterly (CDQ), 2018 (6.4)

To situate the special issues in their disciplinary and historical context, I also collected all the articles that focus on disability and accessibility that appear outside of special issues in the above four journals and in those listed below from the start of their publication history up to the end of 2025 (except for TC, which stopped publication in January 2025):

  • Computers and Composition
  • Journal of Business and Technical Communication
  • Journal of Technical Writing and Communication
  • Kairos
  • Technical Communication Quarterly

To select the articles that focused on disability, I flagged articles with titles that used words such as “disability,” “accessibility,” “handicap,” as well as specific disabilities and disability studies concepts such as “neurodiversity.” When titles did not give me enough information on the main focus of an article, I read the abstract.

Article selection was tricky because the topic of disability is both ubiquitous and a slippery concept in TPC scholarship. Disability appears in articles that discuss usability, user-centered design, document design, mental health, and older adults—without necessarily being the primary focus or even being explicitly labelled as “disability.” It also shows up as metaphor and as an identity marker for users or students. For example, I encountered articles by Peter Cannon and Katie Walkup (2021), which looked at ableist language in mental health documents, and by Danielle De Arment-Donohue (2024), which discussed lactation as a workplace accommodation. Although I hesitated to include them in my dataset, I added them since they focus on ableism and accommodations, two obvious keywords for the topic of disability; they both also use disability studies theory to discuss their findings. In contrast, I immediately added Al Young’s (1990) article titled “The Elephant and the Blind Men: A Look at Functionality” to my set only to remove it later as I realized that it used disability metaphorically and did not focus on it as a topic at all. I decided to include three editorials (Melinda Knight, 2019, 2020, 2021) that focused on disability since they provide some insights on the powers of editors to promote the topic. I also included the introductions to the special issues as they are framing the perspective on disability and accessibility for each issue.

In total, my dataset of articles, editorials, and introductions counts 118 items. However, quantification is not the point of this research project. Instead, I aim at a panoramic illustration of how the topic navigated our field. Thus, I present a qualitative overview of general trends in the scholarship. As my approach is impressionistic and exploratory rather than statistical, it is possible that someone replicating my study would end up with a slightly different dataset. In addition, my positionality as a straight, cis White woman also affects my choices and interpretations, thus making my list of articles reflect my worldview. Therefore, I have included the list of articles I selected to illustrate how I came to my conclusions.

Methodologies of Analysis

I used different methods to analyze the dataset. However, all the methods stemmed from critical discourse analysis, which helps notice ideologies at work in discourse by focusing on the intersections of discourse, language, and power (Yeqing Kong, 2022). Critical discourse analysis is a form of critical reading that investigates

…the often unnoticed details of grammar or word choice… to help the critic understand not only how texts give presence to a particular picture of the world but also how linguistic choices signal one or more of the authors’ possible underlying motives. (Sean Zdenek & Barbara Johnstone, 2008, p. 25)

I performed a qualitative content analysis of my entire dataset, noticing patterns as they arose from the data (Isabelle Thompson, 1999). This helped me distinguish recurring main ideas and concepts in the articles, which stood out by their “intensity and frequency” in the database (Sonja Foss, 2018, p. 413). I also investigated the disability TPC article as a social genre (Carolyn Miller, 1984). That is, I performed a genre analysis of the “TPC article on disability” to uncover how the writers of these articles were both replicating disciplinary ideologies on disability and creating small, incremental changes for how this topic is taken up in our scholarship. Then, to deepen my analysis, I performed a rhetorical analysis of select articles from the special issues to explore some recurring ideas and concepts in more depth.

Throughout the project, I followed critical disability studies methodologies. Since “the critical disability turn addresses ideology, political economy, and cultural systems responsible for characterizing disability as disqualification” (Lichen Hamraie, 2021, p. 240), it offers the best support for rethinking accessibility work as an ideological pushback rather than limiting it to practical aspects such as accommodations and checklists. Additionally, critical disability studies theory dismantles strict binaries of dis/ability since it highlights how everyone is forced to fit within ableist norms. Of course, some people benefit more than others from these ableist norms, leading them at times to accept the status quo and even support it through their actions and discourse. Thus, critical disability studies scholars highlight both how the entire system is constructed through ableist norms that force a simplistic definition of humanity and the power that each person has in terms of supporting this unjust system when it benefits them to do so. The goal, then, is “to interrogate and expose social relations and assumptions such as normalcy and ableism that produce the disabled subject” to free everyone from them, regardless of dis/abilities, and to come to terms with our own participation in buttressing ableist ideologies (Leyton Schnellert et al., 2019, para. 4).

The idea that TPC is complicit in supporting oppressive regimes has long haunted our field (Natasha Jones & Miriam Williams, 2018), but critically analyzing accessibility work in TPC exposes underlying ableism even while we mean to help marginalized populations. Thus, investigating the collaborations between TPC and disability studies reveals how the shifting of values within TPC comes as much from social and ideological changes as from disciplinary soul-searching.

An important strategy from critical disability studies is that of cripping the status quo, which means imagining disability as the rule rather than the exception, centering it instead of leaving it at margins of normativity, and opening up the possibilities of how we could do things differently in our communication practices, pedagogies, and societies (Robert McRuer, 2006). In particular, I use the strategy of crip reading, which means moving disability from the margins of a text to its center to make the text’s normative standards noticeable and therefore highlight their arbitrariness (McRuer, 2006). To notice how disability was written about in the articles on disability, I followed Jan Grue’s (2015) tactic of repeatedly asking “must the issue be framed in this way?” (p. 21). This kind of playfulness with words and of expanding the possibilities for how we could discuss disability is a strategy of crip reading.

This web text summarizes and unites all my findings, which I present through a chronological analysis of the six special issues on disability from four TPC journals, along with some invitations to jump into a few rabbit holes with me (follow the hyperlinks).

Limitations

Focusing on journals led to some limitations:

  • I am excluding from this project other spaces that discuss the topic of disability. Each special issue is connected to the specific context of each journal, not just the changing context of the whole field, which means that the perspective it reveals is more targeted than fully generalizable. Focusing solely on journals also limits my attention to this particular area of the field, excluding other spaces where conversations on disability are happening.
  • My findings are dependent on different journals’ missions, editorial preferences, and calls for papers from the special issues. Goggin (2000) describes journal editors and reviewers as “discipliniographers—those who write the field through authorizing work” (p. xviii), thus underscoring how a privileged few can distort our understanding of what matters to the whole field.
  • Focusing on major TPC publications means focusing on the “systems of power that privilege White, male, able-bodied/minded narratives” (Leah Heilig, 2018, p. 402). Thus, it is important to remember that I am focusing on the voices of the privileged, here, and thus excluding other voices from the dataset.

My hope is that this project will inspire others to investigate the areas that were not part of my scope and continue the conversation. The work is far from done!
