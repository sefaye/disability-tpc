1990–2005: Establishing a Positive Connection Between Disability and TPC



The scholarship on disability in TPC publications never went back to negative themes after Jodie Gilmore’s (1989) article. While we do not have many articles in the 1990s, they are spread across different journals. They focus on paying attention to our language use when we talk about people with disabilities (Lisa Tyler, 1990), argue that lack of accessibility excludes people (Terence Collins, 1990), call on making websites accessible (Marsha Durham, 1999), and signal that people with disabilities have their own culture that must be acknowledged and respected in our classrooms (Gail Wood, 1995). Throughout these articles, we have echoes of the ADA, which was passed in 1990. This act “prohibits employment, access, housing, and educational discrimination against people with disabilities” (Kim Nielsen, 2012, p. 181). All the articles published from this period onward focus on social inclusion, illustrating a switch in TPC towards the social model of disability, which focuses on removing social barriers rather than focusing on “fixing” individuals (Marno Retief & Rantoa Letšosa, 2018).

The 1990s’ more positive and complex perspective on disabilities continues across journals in the early 2000s. We have the first article on disability to appear in Technical Communication Quarterly, which is also the first one to bring in disability studies theory and to explicitly present disability as gain rather than as deficit (James Wilson, 2000). The idea that accessibility helps everyone regardless of dis/abilities comes back frequently in articles from the 2000s (Jeff Carter & Mike Markel, 2001; Patricia Dunn & Kathleen Dunn De Mers, 2002; Susan Grover & Anne Hendricks, 2002; John Slatin, 2001). In 2002, Kairos published a special issue on disability and accessibility, where we have the first acknowledgement of people with disabilities as a diverse population (Grover & Hendricks). Just one year before TC’s 2006 special issue, Mary Theofanos and Janice Redish (2005) published an article in that same journal where they articulated the “paradigm shift” (p. 17), stating that we must include disabled users in our work and center accessibility in all our projects rather than thinking of it as retrofit. The call for the paradigm shift will be recurrent in all future articles on disability in and out of special issues.

We also have two influential articles published in 2004 that, while focused on older adults, call attention to the importance of focusing on forgotten users. In IEEE, Gail Lippincott (2004) published “Gray Matters: Where are the Technical Communicators in Research and Design for Aging Audiences?” This article is well known to anyone doing research on older adults in TPC for its convincing call and practical guidelines on how to include older users in our research and usability tests. This article is cited repeatedly in the articles of TC’s 2006 special issue (Catherine Brys & Wim Vanderbauwhede; Dana Chisnell et al.; Jo Mackiewicz; Thea Van Der Geest), of which Lippincott is a co-guest editor. The second influential article appeared in Technical Communication Quarterly. Karen O’Hara’s (2004) article “‘Curb Cuts’ on the Information Highway: Older Adults and the Internet” presented a recurring disability studies argument that accessibility helps everyone by comparing accessible websites to curb cuts meant for wheelchair riders that end up helping anyone with wheels regardless of dis/abilities. The idea that accessibility helps everyone will become the most recurring concept in the upcoming special issues.
