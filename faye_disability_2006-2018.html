2006–2018: The Interim Period When TPC Became Critical



While there were no special issues on disability and accessibility in TPC publications between 2006 and 2018, this period represents a crucial interim with important articles that set the tone for future TPC research on disability. Two of those notable articles appeared early on in Technical Communication Quarterly (TCQ).

Jason Palmeri (2006) made a strong argument for bringing together TPC, disability studies, and cultural studies, examining how TPCers participate in an ableist society through normative discourse. Palmeri is the first author to alert readers to not just ableist ideologies in the field, but also the involvement of TPCers in supporting them through our work. Palmeri’s argument resonated with Steven Katz’s (1992) warning that the ethics of expediency can lead TPCers to support discrimination. It also adds to the recurring debate over whether TPC should embrace pragmatic or humanistic values (Katherine Adams, 1993; Robert Connors, 2004; Teresa Kynell, 1999; Carolyn Miller, 1979). The ideas that TPC can empower or disempower people through our work, and that we can change ideologies or reinforce them, will return consistently in the upcoming special issues.

The other standout TCQ article is Shannon Walters’ (2010) “Towards an Accessible Pedagogy.” For the first time, we have an explicit union of disability and social justice, calling for making TPC classes accessible. This connection between pedagogy and social justice will be central to BPCQ’s 2018 special issues.

The years between 2013 and 2018 are important in TPC publications focusing on disability. We find foundational work for the field on this topic:

  • Lisa Melonçon’s (2013a) edited collection Rhetorical Accessibility. Melonçon’s introduction called for a deeper connection between disability studies and TPC, as well as more and better research on accessibility. Melonçon (2013b)’s chapter “Toward a Theory of Technological Embodiment” scolded TPC for forgetting about the body and assuming that our users’ bodies are unproblematic. The other chapters in the collection help create a complex understanding of disabilities and are cited often in the special issues.
  • Sushil Sean Oswal’s (2013) article “Exploring Accessibility as a Potential Area of Research for Technical Communication: A Modest Proposal.” Oswal calls on TPC scholars and practitioners to stop thinking of users exclusively as able, to include disabled users in our projects, and to go deeper with our research on disability. Oswal frames accessibility as a social justice responsibility for TPCers because inaccessible workplaces lead to discrimination. Oswal’s (2014) article on participatory design published in CDQ continued this argument by stating that we need to center disabled users in our projects.
  • Kairos publishes a second special issue on disability and accessibility in 2013 (18.1, Accessibility and Multimodal Composition).
  • Oswal and Melonçon’s (2014) “Paying Attention to Accessibility When Designing Online Courses in Technical and Professional Communication.” Published in JBTC, this article called attention to disabled students in TPC programs and the need to make our online classes accessible.
  • Sean Zdenek’s (2015) Reading Sounds. This book on closed captioning joined the call on understanding accessibility as something that helps everyone, emphasizing the need to make accessibility central to our work rather than treat it as retrofit.
  • Oswal and Melonçon’s (2017) “Saying No to the Checklist.” Published in Writing Program Administration, this article reiterated all the ideas listed above, stating that checklists perpetuate normalcy ideologies and that all our projects must center on accessibility and include disabled users at every step.
  • M. Remi Yergeau’s (2018) book Authoring Autism made a profound argument on neuroqueerness to push back on the rigidity of both rhetoric and medicine.

Except for Yergeau, all these authors will become guest editors for special issues on disability in TPC journals. As evident from the list above, these experts repeat the same calls, showing that the arguments for focusing on disability are made and often reiterated because so few scholars actually embrace them in their work and research. However, the momentum here indicates a shift toward the topic of disability as becoming an important one for the field even as we are still fighting disciplinary and social inertia.
