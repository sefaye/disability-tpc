Technical Communication (TC) 2006 Special Issue

Accessibility and the Technical Communication Profession (53.1)



The first special issue to focus on accessibility in TPC publications started with an introduction by Gail Lippincott and Kathryn Riley encouraging more research on disability. This call is repeated in later special issues’ introductions, along with a pronounced scolding for the lack of research on this topic (Melinda Knight & Sushil Oswal, 2018; Lisa Melonçon & Nupoor Ranade, 2021; Sushil Oswal, 2018). TC’s introduction contained the first mention of disabled TPCers, both as professionals and academics, and this subtopic will be taken up again in BPCQ’s 2018 and IEEE’s 2021 special issues. Lippincott and Riley also went over basic disability studies concepts and keywords, such as the curb cut argument that presents accessibility as helping everyone.

This issue’s articles are how-to texts (Nancy Bayer & Lisa Pappas; Catherine Brys & Wim Vanderbauwhede; Linda Enders Roberts; Thea van Der Geest), guidelines on ensuring online accessibility (Dana Chisnell et al.), or annotated bibliographies on accessibility resources (Jo Mackiewicz), each emphasizing the role of technology in granting or removing access (from a practical rather than political standpoint). Indeed, these articles invoked the ADA for its legal requirements but did not engage critically with its shortcomings. Lippincott and Riley stated that this special issue was a “snapshot of knowledge at a particular moment in time” (p. 13). All the articles focused on how to make websites accessible, reflecting an interest in the prominent technology of the time. The authors, though, also tended to represent accessibility as something practical that “fixes” disability through fixing technology. These authors, while demonstrating goodwill and interest in the topic of disability, might have leaned towards “technoableism,” the idea that technology can fix all problems related to disability (Lichen Hamraie, 2023, p. 305).

However, these 2006 TC articles are also extremely different from IEEE’s 1985 articles, as they insisted on the importance of including disabled users in usability tests, providing detailed guidelines on how to set up those tests successfully and how to make convincing arguments with clients that creating a budget for these tests is money well spent (Bayer & Pappas; van Der Geest). These articles also acknowledged that accessibility helps everyone (Bayer & Pappas; Brys & Vanderbauwhede; Chisnell et al.; Roberts; van Der Geest) since easier website navigation helps any user. Finally, we find a careful attention to the embodied experience of disabled users through the argument that accessibility should lead to a pleasurable experience for disabled users rather than just granting practical access (Roberts; van Der Geest).

While these articles, with their focus on including disabled users in usability tests, are moving the field towards the paradigm shift, they are not presenting a full shift yet. For example, van der Geest’s article “Conducting Usability Studies With Users Who Are Elderly or Have Disabilities,” offers guidelines on setting up effective usability tests with disabled and older adult users, stating that “usability tests with users who are disabled or elderly have resulted in Web Sites that at least offer a better experience for all users” (p. 30). However, van der Geest did not fully commit to the paradigm shift since she stated that it is not always necessary to include disabled or older adults users: “whether these groups should be included in usability tests depends on the nature and purpose of a specific site,” and if the information in these sites is “relevant” to people with mobility impairments (p. 24). This hesitation to always include disabled users even though the test results demonstrate an improvement for all users illustrates that van der Geest was navigating in a society (and perhaps a field) that was far from having normalized the idea that disability is an important aspect of human diversity.

Even though they might not have achieved a full paradigm shift, the articles in this special issue paved the way for a more engaged binding between disability and TPC, as is made evident by the publications between this and the next special issues.

The next special issues appear in 2018 in BPCQ and CDQ, in a world and a field profoundly changed.
