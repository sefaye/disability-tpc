Between the Special Issues and 2025: Are the Calls Finally Answered?



At the time of publication of this web-text, the amount of research on disability seems promising; we can find numerous articles in different journals on this topic that appear outside of special issues. In the selection of articles below, we can find both positive and negative trends on how the topic continues to navigate our field.

In a positive trend, we find authors picking up and continuing the work from the special issues:

  • Jared Colton et al. (2023) explored the concept that accessibility helps everyone.
  • S. Cavar & ulysses/constance bougie (2024) illustrated disability as gain through their neurodiverse collaboration.
  • Ann Amicucci (2024), Philip Gallagher and Marci Gallagher (2024), and Leah Heilig et al. (2024) brought new complexity to accessibility practices by avoiding the checklist and discussing novel critical perspectives and practices.

The COVID pandemic also brought more attention to disability as concerns grew for marginalized populations:

  • Jianfen Chen et al. (2023): “Reimagining student-centered learning: Accessible and inclusive syllabus design during and after the COVID-19 pandemic,” Computers and Composition.
  • Heidi Skurat Harris, et al. (2022): “What the COVID pandemic taught us about creating inclusive, anti-racist, and accessible online writing classes and programs,” Computers and Composition.
  • Ashleen Walden (2022): “Necessity is the mother of invention: Accessibility pre, inter, & post pandemic,” Computers and Composition.

A problematic trend is finding the same names from editors or authors of the special issues articles appearing as the dominant voices continuing those conversations, often repeating the same calls:

  • Kristin Bennett (2022) called disability a social justice concern and warns TPC to stop privileging one population over others.
  • Bennett and Mark Hannah (2022) focused on rights and disability justice, highlighting the danger of further marginalizing marginalized populations through technical communication practices that just focus on the legally mandated.
  • Bennett (2023) called attention to TPC’s continued tendency to think of discourse as neutral and to ignore disability’s intersectionality.
  • Bennett (2024) revealed ableist ideologies that are embedded in the concept of professionalism in university career centers.
  • Bennett (2025) warned against the danger of mHealth apps as surveillance tools.
  • Sushil Oswal and Zsuzsanna Palmer (2021) called for participatory design where disabled users are included in the creation of our projects.
  • Palmer et al. (2021) described three courses linked through collaboration and a particular attention to accessibility.
  • Palmer & Oswal (2025) warned that AI-made websites are not fully accessible.
  • Adam Strantz (2024) discussed how to incorporate accessibility into design courses.

The frequent recurrence of similar names underscores that the topic still resides heavily among TPC experts on disability. Also, the field’s research approaches on disability had not gone through a rigorous enough change since these authors have to reiterate the same special issues’ calls for TPC to improve its scholarship on disability.

While there are more articles on disability, we are still struggling as a field to meet basic calls such as the paradigm shift of including disabled users in our practices and research. Willers (2024) returned to the paradigm shift and highlighted a claim also found in Oswal and Palmer (2021): The field still has not made this shift. This trend resembles Kim E. Nielsen’s (2012) evaluation of the ADA as not always being enforced, practiced, or supported by courts and in danger of eroding and disappearing without activists’ constant pushback. Sadly, we can notice something similar with the topic of disability in TPC: We claim loudly that we value accessibility, but, as this chronology shows, the crucial work of a few activist-scholars in keeping this topic in our journals is not representative of what happens in the rest of the field.
