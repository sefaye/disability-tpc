Models of Disability

Following the topic of disability within TPC publications reveals changes in the theoretical background that inform how disability and accessibility are examined and described. Here, I’ve put the articles from special issues on disability and accessibility in conversation with the disability studies theories that were most prominent during their sociohistorical context.

As a topic, disability entered TPC publications during the 1980s and 1990s, with a focus on web access and retrofit accommodations. It then began to shift slowly toward the more complex idea of disability as an important aspect of human life that needs to be included in participatory design projects (Leah Heilig, 2023). This shift parallels the changing models of disability, which are perspectives on disability that have material consequences on how we do accessibility work.

At first, TPC followed the medical model, “reducing every aspect of disability to bodily impairment, prescribing only medical treatment and normalization as appropriate interventions” (Jan Grue, 2015, p. 38). Also known as “the ‘personal tragedy’ model” because disability inspires pity, this understanding of disability offers no positive perspective on disabilities and “treat[s] people as problems to be solved” (Marno Retief & Rantoa Letšosa, 2018, p. 3). Indeed, Grue explained that this model is “reducing every aspect of disability to bodily impairment, prescribing only medical treatment and normalization as appropriate interventions” (p. 38) and described how this model is highly criticized in disability studies. This deficit model is most prominent in the IEEE 1985 special issue where the goal of TPC was to fix (that is, erase) disability through accommodations that targeted disabilities as problems in need of solutions.

Then, the field turned to the social model of disability, which is still present in TPC today, with its focus on removing social barriers rather than “fixing” individuals (Retief & Letšosa, 2018). While less ableist than the medical model, the social model’s focus on social factors of exclusion can also lead to forgetting about embodiment and the intersectionality of disability (Grue, 2015). Thus, scholars merging disability studies and TPC today tend to move toward the critical social model. This model pushes back against the social model’s simplistic perspective of disability as being socially created through physical barriers and prefers a perspective on disability as a complex phenomenon that merges social, cultural, intersectional, and embodied experiences (Kathy Boxall, 2019). What is more, the critical social model allows for much more complex definitions of what it means to be a human being because of its deconstruction of the labels “able” vs. “disabled,” thus highlighting how binary thinking forces everyone to fit normative standards (Leyton Schnellert et al., 2019).

It is important to remember, though, that these disability models overlap and are not mutually exclusive. All of these “models become embedded in discourse and discourse practices, which means that their meaning often changes according to who is using them, and for what purposes” (Grue, p. 124). The overlap in models can help us understand some contrasting trends and directions in the articles of the special issues, especially those situated in periods where the field was undergoing a shift in its values.

The critical social model, which is so prominent in the later special issues on disability and accessibility, comes from a shift from “disability studies” to “critical disability studies.” Critical disability studies is an approach to studying disability that critiques simplistic previous models of disability, along with biomedical and individual representations of disability, to focus instead on the social structures that create the binary thinking of disabled versus nondisabled (Schnellert et al., 2019). Or, as Rosemarie Garland-Thomson (2019) described it, critical disability studies

…expands the understanding of disability from a health science perspective to consider it as a civil and human rights issue, a minority identity, a sociological formation, a historic community, a diversity group, and a category of critical analysis in culture and the arts. (p. 12)

Further, Schnellert et al. (2019) offered this analogy: “In the same ways that critical White studies and queer studies interrupt White privilege and heteronormativity, critical disability studies seeks to interrupt the privileging of normative practices and perspectives that produce both the enabled and disabled subjects” (para. 10). By critically analyzing how both nondisabled and disabled body/minds have been ascribed ableist definitions, critical disability studies scholars push back against the normative entanglements that separate and hierarchize people. In that way, critical disability studies scholarship is similar to intersectional feminism. By trying to end sexist oppression for all, intersectional feminists aim at liberating everyone, regardless of gender, race, class, or sexual orientation, from the constrictive ideology of gender norms (bell hooks, 1984).

Similarly, critical disability studies scholars work towards “understanding how disability operates as an identity category and cultural concept” that helps us “understand what it is to be human, our relationships with one another, and the experience of embodiment” (Garland-Thomson, 2002, p. 5). Additionally, critical disability studies calls for self-scrutinizing to understand how some of us may be profiting from ableist structures and unwittingly supporting them when we find ourselves in the hegemonic side of the dis/ability binary (Schnellert et al., 2019).
