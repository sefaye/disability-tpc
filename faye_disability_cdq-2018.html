Communication Design Quarterly (CDQ) 2018 Special Issue

“Reimagining Disability and Accessibility in Technical and Professional Communication” (6.4)



We find a third special issue on disability and accessibility in 2018, this time in CDQ. Much like Sushil Oswal in BPCQ, this special issue’s guest editor, Sean Zdenek, scolded the TPC field for still imagining all our users as nondisabled and calls for transforming our practices. Citing Mary Frances Theofanos and Janice (Ginny) Redish’s (2005) article urging for “a paradigm shift” (p. 5), he stated that next to nothing had changed since then in terms of putting disability at the center of our practices. He framed disability studies as having a “transformative potential” (p. 4) for TPC as it provides important insights for the field. Positioning accessibility as a civil right, Zdenek’s introduction went deep into disability studies theory to argue against socially constructed norms.

An important distinction from Oswal’s (2018) BPCQ introduction, though, is that Zdenek was not providing a disability studies primer for readers—expecting them to already have some theoretical knowledge—but was instead using disability studies theory for supporting a complex scholarly argument on “disability studies as a critical methodology” (p. 8). This difference in expectations indicated a notable difference between the two journals. While BPCQ has practically no disability articles published before its special issues, CDQ had already started this conversation with its readers throughout the years preceding the special issue (Hinson, 2017; Moeller, 2015; Oswal, 2013, 2014, 2015). Thus, if the 2018 BPCQ 81.1 special issue offered a crash course on disability studies, CDQ’s special issue presented a deeper reflection on the connections between social justice, accessibility, and disability studies.

Indeed, the CDQ articles focus on social justice (Laura Gonzales; Sherena Huntsman et al.), the paradigm shift (Huntsman et al.), disability as a gain for TPC (Kevin Garrison; Gonzales), the intersectionality of disability (Gonzales), and accessibility as helping everyone (Garrison; Gonzales). All the authors revealed a complex perspective on the TPC–disability studies relationship. For example, Garrison investigated what lip reading can teach TPC about interface design, while Gonzales brought together intersectionality and interdependency to argue for the added benefits of merging accessibility with language diversity. Both authors thus presented disability as something that would deepen the work of TPC.

For example, in the article “Theorizing Lip Reading as Interface Design: The Gadfly of the Gaps,” Garrison framed lip reading as a strategy that can “offer technical communicators insights into communication, such as interface design” (p. 26). Garrison called for more research on this lesser-known topic and urged the field to stop imagining the users as hearing and instead to think of “lip reading as an area of potential insight and further research for enriching our ideas about communication [and the] gaps within other forms of communication, especially the hybridized forms of multimedia communication” (p. 32). Garrison transformed the concept of accessibility into something much more complex than what we found in the TC articles, shifting how we think about disability in our field. This article urged us to turn our attention to how material disabled experiences enrich everyone’s understanding of communication. For Garrison, TPCers must learn from people with disabilities and weave these insights into our practices.

Accessibility for Social Justice

The call for social change appeared throughout this issue, with authors calling for TPCers to become agents of change against institutional ableism. Huntsman et al.’s article, “Cultivating Virtuous Course Designers: Using Technical Communication to Reimagine Accessibility in Higher Education,” described the coalition of a PhD student, an accessibility coordinator, and a TPC assistant professor who intended to broaden accessibility perspectives and practices on their campus. The authors called for practicing virtue ethics and articulated the need for courage to push for accessibility in environments that might resist that change. Rather than following regulations and requirements, virtue ethics “prioritizes the moral dispositions that individuals develop through their daily practices” with the aim of cultivating “patience, empathy, humility, and generosity” in the community (p. 13). Huntsman et al. urged TPCers to take on the role of Aristotle’s “exemplars,” or what Confucius would describe as “people who exemplify the virtues” (p. 20), and “view themselves—at different times in their lives—on the spectrums of justice and courage” (p. 21). Huntsman et al. set a very high standard for TPC work that bears little resemblance to the idea of accessibility work as focusing on clear writing (e.g., IEEE 1985) or on fixing technology (e.g., TC 2006).

However, recapping the three 2018 special issues in BPCQ and CDQ, we find an uncomfortable dichotomy. On one side, we have hopeful shifts in scholarship and flourishing field experts guiding the field’s conversations on dismantling systemic ableism through our students’ and our own work. These efforts make disability an important TPC topic that enhances our practices and research, uniting accessibility and social justice. On the other side, we have guest editors and article authors continuously warning the field that we are not researching this topic enough and that we are far from having achieved the paradigm shift on how we should be doing accessibility in TPC. A telling example of this dichotomy comes from CDQ’s 2018 special issue. At the end of his introduction to the special issue, Zdenek added a note that stated:

the issue’s contributors carefully prepared their Word documents to be accessible when converted to PDFs by including alt text for figures and semantic tagging for headings. Access to these features was lost when the Word files were formatted to the journal’s specifications. (p. 9)

He then gave his contact information to anyone who would want “versions of the articles from this special issue that have been optimized for screen reader accessibility” (p. 9). There is no better example of TPC’s internal struggles to change its practices even while calling for change. There is hope that things are progressing in more useful directions, though. Since then, CDQ requests its authors to follow a “A best-practices guide to accessible document design” to create accessible documents; even further, all articles set to be published in CDQ go through additional procedures to ensure that they are accessible (Jordan Frith, personal communication, February 14, 2025).

After these three 2018 special issues and a short three-year interim period from 2018 to 2021, we find one last special issue on this topic published by IEEE in 2021—and one more echo of the call for a paradigm shift.
