IEEE Transactions on Professional Communication (IEEE) 2021 Special Issue

Special Issue on Accessibility (64.3)



IEEE evolved greatly between the special issue of 1985 and 2021, widening its intended audience to include TPC researchers, instructors, and practitioners while becoming a journal “devoted to applied research on professional communication—including but not limited to technical and business communication” (Procomm, 2025b, para. 1). The context for the 2021 issue was also very different from the BPCQ’s and CDQ’s 2018 issues. Even though IEEE had not published an article on disability since Jeff Carter and Mike Markel’s piece in 2001, by then there were quite a few articles on disability in TPC publications because of the three 2018 special issues and other publications from 2018 to 2021. If Sushil Oswal’s introduction in BPCQ’s 81.1 issue scolded the field for not knowing the basics of disability studies and not publishing on the topic, Lisa Melonçon and Nupoor Ranade’s 2021 introduction in IEEE furthered the critique by recalling all the work that had been done by TPC scholars focusing on disability and that remained mostly ignored in the field’s theory and practices.

The introduction by Melonçon and Ranade focused on the paradigm shift needed to center accessibility in TPC, abandon checklists, and included accessibility at every step of our practices and research. They stated:

To achieve accessibility means that technical and professional communicators… have to engage with a paradigm shift. That paradigm shift involves a change in culture within organizations and throughout the processes of making so that accessibility and all that it entails is considered at the start and in every step of any project. (p. 216)

As we have noticed, this call has been made repeatedly (e.g., from Mary Frances Theofanos & Janice (Ginny) Redish in 2005 to Sean Zdenek in 2018) but had still not been adopted by the whole field. Many of the themes in the introduction were repeated in the articles:

  • accessibility is a rhetorical endeavor (Kristin Bennett & Mark Hannah)
  • disability is a gain for TPC and accessibility helps everyone (Sherena Huntsman; Kate Sonka et al.)
  • accessibility needs to be more than a legal requirement since the ADA falls short of creating truly inclusive spaces (Bennett & Hannah)
  • accessibility is an important topic in TPC (Huntsman; Adam Strantz)
  • disability is an ignored topic in our research; there is not enough specialization in disability studies (Bennett & Hannah; Strantz); and there is a lack of research on the subtopics of disability and accessibility (Matthew Baker et al.; Christin Phelps)

The authors of this issue scolded the field for learning only the basics of disability studies and for being content to “just provide the bare minimum of accessibility” (Strantz, p. 289) and to rely on “formulaic responses” and “a one-size-fits-all” (Huntsman, pp. 221–222). The critique in these articles expanded to the core areas of TPC: academia (Strantz), professional editing (Baker et al.), industry (Bennett & Hannah; Sonka et al.), and teaching (Phelps). Seeking to influence the field, this issue of IEEE called for TPC to go beyond doing the minimum: We must specialize in disability studies and think of disability in much more complex terms (Huntsman; Strantz; Sonka et al.). Each of the articles in this issue focused on a subtopic overlapping TPC and disability, going from the invisible work of using adaptive technology (Baker et al.) to accessible visualizations (Strantz), to understudied disabilities such as dyslexia (Phelps), as well as the rich insights we gain when we merge TPC and disability studies (Bennett & Hannah; Huntsman; Sonka et al.). All presented the topic of disability as crucial for doing optimal TPC work.

For example, Baker et al. highlighted the invisible work added by adaptive technology during collaborative editing tasks and argued that the field needs to understand the added work that our projects demand for those using adaptive technology. They offered a tutorial for blind editors on common editing tasks and share the experience of one of the authors who is blind. The tutorial for blind editors, which may seem focused on a very specific target audience, could be useful for sighted TPCers as well since “being aware of these editing processes and their implications can help sighted teachers and practitioners better appreciate and accommodate their blind or visually impaired students’ and colleagues’ work” (p. 285). Calling for a more sophisticated approach to thinking about the use of technology by disabled users, this article argued that knowing about the work related to using assistive technology gives TPC important insight on how to improve our practices. There was a sense of poetic justice that this article appeared in IEEE’s 2021 special issue, illustrating the radical shift that the journal and the field have gone through since IEEE’s 1985 special issue on “disadvantaged readers” (Valerie Arms, p. 1).

Another example of how the authors from this issue perceive disability as a fundamental aspect of doing good TPC work is the merging of accessibility with social justice. Right before this special issue, Rebecca Walton et al. (2019) published Technical Communication After the Social Justice Turn, highlighting the importance of social justice for TPC. Thus, it is not surprising to find the argument that accessibility is about rights and social justice in the introduction and in its articles (Bennett & Hannah; Huntsman; Sonka et al.). The authors reminded readers that TPC can either reproduce barriers or remove them (Bennett & Hannah; Huntsman; Phelps; Sonka et al.) and call for a humanistic approach in TPC (Sonka et al.). They invited us to pay attention “to the nuances of disabled experiences” (Bennett & Hannah, p. 247), to tune in to “forward-looking accessibility… so no audience is left unable” (Strantz, p. 299), and to realize that “we as accessibility advocates must alter our tactics” if we want to instigate social change (Huntsman, p. 233).

Continuing the argument found in BPCQ’s 2018 articles, Sonka et al. (2021) called on bringing social justice as a topic in the classroom since they want students to understand TPC work as the “work [of] advocates and allies” (p. 264), since “resituating accessibility work within a social justice design framework helps teams focus on improving industry from within and rebuilding it with shared values of diversity, equity, and inclusion” (p. 267). Some authors also warned the field that our work is often ableist even when we mean well (Bennett & Hannah; Strantz). For example, Bennet and Hannah warned that if TPCers “promote normative efficiency that positions disability as pathology” (p. 235), they “are potentially implicated in discourse that contributes to disability’s erasure” (p. 238). Thus, they called for scrutiny—of ourselves and of our practices and theory—to truly change the status quo. These authors understood social justice and accessibility as something that TPCers do at every step of the process in our classes, practices, theory, and research projects. Therefore, to create projects that are anti-ableist, these authors called for connecting social justice and accessibility with disciplinary and professional soul searching.
