Business and Professional Communication Quarterly (BPCQ) 2018: Two issues focusing on disability and accessibility

“Can Workplaces, Classrooms, and Pedagogies be Disabling?” (81.1)
“Disability and Accessibility in the Workplace” (81.4)



Twelve years after the TC 2006 special issue, we find two special issues dedicated to disability and accessibility in BPCQ appearing in 2018. These issues (81.1 and 81.4) are a bit of a surprise since BPCQ published almost nothing on this topic prior to 2018. While the 1975 how-to in BPCQ was the first mention of disabilities in any journal of the field, we only find one other article (Lisa Tyler, 1990) on disabilities published in this journal before its 2018 special issues on disability and accessibility, leaving pre-2018 BPCQ trailing behind other journals in terms of publication on this topic.

81.1. “Can Workplaces, Classrooms, and Pedagogies be Disabling?”

In the 81.1 issue, guest editor Sushil Oswal scolded at length both the journal and the TPC field for ignoring the topic of disability in its publications. In fact, the first article in this issue showcased the inaccessibility of our conferences and websites and the lack of research on disability in our publications (Melinda Knight). Oswal criticized TPC for ignoring both disabled users and disabled TPCers and celebrated that this issue stands out for having many disabled authors (indeed, of all the special issues, BPCQ 81.1 focuses the most on TPCers with disabilities).

In the introduction, Oswal introduced many concepts from disability studies, such as the intersectionality of disability to create a more complex perspective on disability, the disabling structures and ideologies that TPC can help create and reinforce, the critical social model of disability that positions disability as an important part of human experience, and terminology for discussing people with disabilities. This extensive review of disability studies theory shows that Oswal did not trust the readers of BPCQ to know much about disability studies or, at least, he wanted to level the playing field by bringing all readers up to speed. Indeed, in the 81.4 introduction, Knight and Oswal described the 81.1 issue as “a crash course on disability and accessibility theory” (Knight & Oswal, 2018, p. 395).

The 81.1 issue focused on the workplace and on TPC courses. Its articles called for incorporating disability as a theoretical framework in our pedagogy (Danielle Nielsen) and making our classes accessible (Allison Hitt; Lisa Melonçon; Danielle Nielsen). Many authors continued Jason Palmeri’s (2006) argument that teaching our students about disability and accessibility helps bring change to the workplace (Geoffrey Clegg; Annika Konrad; Melonçon; Stephanie Wheeler). The goal was no longer to fix the technology like in the TC 2006 special issue articles, but instead to train our students to be advocates for people with disabilities and to combat systemic ableism. This sense of mission was a hopeful one as it called on the field to change the unjust social status quo through the training of future TPCers.

81.4 “Disability and Accessibility in the Workplace”

Extending the concept of TPC’s potential to change society, the second BPCQ special issue of 2018 focused on laws. Again, the authors posited that our students can be trained to create systemic change, but to do so, they must also understand the legal system surrounding disabilities. The introduction, penned by guest editor Oswal and editor Knight, as well as the three feature articles, spent considerable time introducing and explaining disability rights. Zsuzsanna Bacsa Palmer and Ralph Palmer positioned accessibility as a human right and highlighted the lack of legal protection against inaccessible websites. They called for including legal studies in our courses and engaging critically with the ADA because its language on online accessibility is too ambiguous. Similarly, Leah Heilig highlighted the ineffectiveness of Web Content Accessibility Guidelines to make interactive maps accessible, and Norman Youngblood and Michael Brooks claim it is TPC’s ethical duty to make accessible health websites for veterans. The legal focus of the 81.4 issue was almost a special topic within a special issue, making it a crash course in disability law to, again, bring BPCQ’s readers up to speed.

Because both issues presented similar types of critique and focus on pedagogy, I am discussing all their articles together in the following analysis.

The Field’s Increasing Expertise

The articles published in the two 2018 BPCQ special issues cited numerous publications that bring together TPC and disability, mostly from the interim period between TC’s 2006 special issue and the BPCQ’s 2018 special issues (e.g., Melonçon, 2013a; Oswal & Melonçon, 2014, 2017; Sean Zdenek, 2015, 2009; Shannon Walters, 2010; Palmeri, 2006; Oswal, 2013). Thus, they showed how the field is building internal expertise on the connections between disability and TPC. At the end of the special issues’ introductions and articles, it is implied that while the readers of the journal might have been ignorant before these special issues, they now have no excuses and cannot continue to ignore the topic of disability.

In both of the 2018 issues, the authors were more politically engaged than the 2006 TC authors: All the articles critiqued our inaccessible society and TPC’s ignorance on disability and accessibility. They stated that research on disability and accessibility was “entirely absent” (Knight, p. 29) or “still often absent” (Hitt, p. 53) in our field, that “little research has been done on disability studies and professional communication” (Nielsen, p. 71), and scholars “have been slow” (Melonçon, p. 34) to pick it up. Even further, “no journal articles about accessibility exist in business communication” (Palmer & Palmer, p. 400) and “no disciplinary research exists on the topic” of accessible presentations (Clegg, p. 102). The writers gave extensive explanations on disability studies concepts and keywords in lengthy literature reviews that cover work by disability studies scholars and TPC scholars who have built expertise in this topic. The authors also consistently presented positive perspectives on disability by arguing that focusing on disability can be a gain for TPC (Clegg; Hitt; Melonçon) and that accessibility helps everyone (Hitt; Nielsen).

Training Our Students to Change the World

Since BPCQ is “devoted to research that advances the teaching of communication in the workplace” (BPCQ, 2025, para. 1), it makes sense that the authors focused on how to teach accessibility to TPC students. Nielsen called on instructors to teach how to “[design] for disability first, not the norm” (p. 80). Similarly, Melonçon “offered a new theory—orienting access—to encourage instructors to reorient their pedagogies in such way that they can create accessible learning environments… [and] as an opportunity to create learning spaces that engage students in new ways” (p. 48). The authors described teaching practices that go from creating assignments requiring in-depth audience analysis (Nielsen, 2018) and analyzing accessibility strategies such as universal design (Hitt) to connecting students with disabled individuals through community-based work (Clegg) and having students use tools for checking the accessibility of websites to help them notice accessibility issues (Heilig; Youngblood & Brooks). Other strategies included changing students’ perspectives on accessibility by connecting ethics and civic engagement with what we do in the classroom (Palmer & Palmer; Wheeler) and changing the language in syllabi to make students aware of the importance of accessibility (Melonçon).

All the authors argued that we need to bring disability studies into TPC classrooms to train students who will then create systemic change in the workplace. For example, Palmer & Palmer stated that “having gained a strong ethical conviction that being able to access websites is a human right, our business communication students might become accessibility champions in their prospective workplaces” (p. 416). Hitt argued that centering accessibility in our courses is vital to “prepare students to be ethical professional communicators and for ensuring the accessibility of our pedagogies for disabled and nondisabled students’ own diverse accessibility needs” (p. 53). Indeed, teaching universal design critically “repositions accessibility not just as a legal requirement but as a socially just, ethical commitment to creating a rich rhetorical experience for a range of diverse users” (p. 62). We find in these articles a depiction of TPCers as changing the world by being metaphorical gardeners: Through our students, we can spread the seeds that will rectify the ecology of the whole ecosystem.

In the same year of the two BPCQ special issues, we have a third special issue focusing on the topic of disability and accessibility in CDQ.
