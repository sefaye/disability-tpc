Word Choices

I define disability as an embodied experience that responds to the constructed world around us (Rosemarie Garland-Thomson, 2021) and as an integral aspect of the complexity of human life (H-Dirksen Bauman & Joseph J. Murray, 2010). Taking a cue from Katherine Durack (2004), who argued against rigid definitions of aspects of technical writing—they tend to exclude the work of technical and professional communicators (TPCers) that does not fit a strict definition—I use a purposely vague definition of disability to include all types of dis/abilities and different perspectives on disability.

While the main focus of my discussion is the topic of disability, I use both the single word “disability” and the phrase “disability and accessibility” to include different wording and main focuses within the special issues. For example, IEEE’s 1985 special issue uses neither term but frames the topic as “disadvantaged readers” (Valerie Arms, 1985, p. 1). Both IEEE’s 2021 and TC’s 2006 issues use only the keyword “accessibility” to indicate their focus, while CDQ’s 2018 and BPCQ’s 2018 issue (81.4) both use “disability and accessibility” in their introduction titles. The title of BPCQ’s 81.1 issue is “Can Workplaces, Classrooms, and Pedagogies be Disabling?”

I intentionally use both disability-first and person-first language, applying the terms “people/users with disabilities” and “disabled people/users” interchangeably. I also switch between disability and impairment (such as “blind” and “visually impaired”). All these terms are used in disability studies (Sushil Oswal, 2019) and reflect different preferences in terms of identity.

Additionally, I use the term dis/ability to avoid the binary distinction in the phrase “disability and ability,” which presents the two terms as if they were irretrievably separated rather than constantly moving on the same continuum (Robert McRuer, 2006). I also use the plural dis/abilities for the same reasons. Some scholars prefer spelling dis/ability as (dis)ability, as can be found in Kristin Bennett (2024), but I prefer dis/ability as the use of parentheses might be misinterpreted as indicating a hierarchy between the two terms. Finally, because disability studies has a history of overfocusing on physical rather than cognitive or intellectual disabilities (Faye Ginsburg & Rayna Rapp, 2018), I use the term body/minds when I mean every type of disability and want to minimize the separation between body and mind (Margaret Price, 2015); I use the forward slash to follow a similar construction as dis/ability.

In this web-text, the word field only indicates TPC academic scholarship. I am focusing on academia rather than industry because I am interested in continuing the work of scholars who have dismantled the idea that academia is a neutral, inclusive space that does not need further investigation in its commitment to welcoming all through its doors. Scholars in disability studies have underscored the clash between academia’s words of inclusion and its eugenic foundations of a hierarchy among human beings (Jay Dolmage, 2017, 2018; Sharon Snyder & David Mitchell, 2005). In ways similar to imposed heterosexuality, academia builds itself as a place of “able-bodied hegemony, figuratively and literally constructing a world that always and everywhere privileges very narrow (and ever-narrowing) conceptions of ability” (McRuer, 2006, p. 151). Sara Ahmed (2012) argued that universities can be satisfied with the performance of speaking words of inclusion without actually translating that performance into inclusion. Thus, universities become spaces that pretend to include everyone while solidifying the roadblocks for those they do not want to include. In fact, because academia systematically devalues people with disabilities (Dolmage, 2008, 2017; Ada Hubrig, 2023; Stephanie Kerschbaum, 2014; Simi Linton, 1998; Price, 2011) and transforms disabled scholars into misfits doing work deemed useless by the institution (Ahmed, 2019; Garland-Thomson, 2011), it hinders their efforts to combat institutional ableism, thus perpetuating the idea that disabilities have no place in the university.
