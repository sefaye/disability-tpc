“No Real Handicap to Me”

The earliest mention of disability I could find in TPC publications appeared in 1975 in BPCQ (at the time called Business Communication Quarterly): “Four Ways of Explaining a Physical Handicap in the Application Letter and Resume.” Actually, it is not an article but an authorless, one-page how-to presenting excerpts of job application letters from TPC students with disabilities, where the students acknowledged their disabilities but downplayed them to convince employers that they would be able to perform their work duties.

The how-to offered four snippets taken from application letters and resumes of people who have provided explanations of their physical disability to their future employers. No additional information is provided. The writers of the snippets were paraplegic students, and the how-to was meant to help faculty advisors who want to counsel students with disabilities during their job application process. In fact, the how-to started with: “Sometimes it is hard to counsel the handicapped student who wants to know whether or not he should mention his handicap” (p. 20).

The excerpts show a complex disability disclosing/erasing rhetorical strategy. The paraplegic students disclosed their disability by stating that they were “confined to a wheelchair,” but quickly added that they were “in excellent health.” They also minimized their disability by highlighting the fact that it only affected their legs: their poliomyelitis “resulted only in paralysis of both legs” and “the only paralysis I suffered was in my legs” (p. 20). The insistence to localize the “handicap” in the legs and to minimize it with “only” revealed some disdain towards the lower body—a Cartesian separation of mind and body, where the mind is all that matters to perform the job. Even further, the hyperfocus on legs highlighted that their disability was not cognitive, a clear sign of a distrust towards cognitive disabilities—still a prevalent feeling in society and a feeling reinforced by the disability movement itself due to its past tendency to privilege attention on physical disabilities (David Wasserman, 2022).

After they minimized their disability, the job seekers switched to another rhetorical strategy, asserting their ability to work—or rather, their ability to excel at work: The disability “has not impaired either my personality or my willingness to work”; “You will find me eager to take advantage of every opportunity to grow in usefulness to you”; “I am certain I can handle the job satisfactorily”; “I feel I am quite capable of doing this type of work even though I am in a wheelchair. This has proved no real handicap to me”; “It is considered a liability, by some, to be in a wheelchair, but I consider it an asset…. It has made it necessary for me to work a little harder than the next fellow”; “I don’t claim any extra abilities, but I have an intense desire to prove to myself and to those who say it cannot be done that I can be a success” (p. 20).

This how-to was published in a pre-ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act) world, which means that in 1975 job seekers had very few avenues of legal protection against employment discrimination since the language of the ADA about providing “reasonable accommodation” had not yet become a legal mandate (Elizabeth Emens, 2015; Kim Nielsen, 2012). Therefore, people with disabilities had to make themselves accommodable to their future employers. This made the rhetorical needs to persuade employers all the more important: The effective argument won the job while the ineffective one left the person unemployed. Therefore, it is not surprising that all the snippet writers adopt rhetorical strategies that highlight their “ability” to work “despite” (or at times reinforced) by their disability. In short, they have to perform a rhetorical tour de force where they are naming, erasing, and reframing their disability in one sentence.

After this brief 1975 how-to in BPCQ, there are no articles focusing on disability in TPC publications until IEEE’s 1985 special issue.
