Recurring Ideas and Concepts

As I read articles spanning decades, I noticed recurring concepts and ideas about disability. I offer here some quick definitions and examples of the most recurring ones.

Accessibility Helps Everyone

This is the most recurrent concept in the articles on disability. This concept means that by rendering information accessible for the purpose of improving its access to people with disabilities, we make the information easier to access for everyone. There is an interesting shift in meaning for this concept, though, as it gains in complexity across the years. While most of the articles in TC’s 2006 special issue underscored that easing navigation would benefit any website user (Nancy Bayer & Lisa Pappas, Catherine Brys & Wim Vanderbauwhede, Dana Chisnell et al., Linda Enders Roberts), later articles in the special issues of other journals gave more complex explanations of what “helps everyone” means: By going beyond mere legal requirements, companies can increase their customer base (Sherena Huntsman, 2021; Danielle Nielsen, 2018; Zsuzsanna Bacsa Palmer and Ralph Palmer, 2018); merging accessibility with linguistic diversity makes our projects accessible to wider audiences (Laura Gonzales, 2018); and accessibility helps us meet the various learning needs of a diverse population and thereby improves our teaching practices (Kate Sonka et al., 2021). Even further, other articles (Kristin Bennett & Mark Hannah, 2021; Adam Strantz, 2021) connected this concept with changing the status quo. We can dismantle oppressive systems and liberate (i.e., “help”) everyone, regardless of dis/ability, when we center accessibility in our practices. This call transcends superficial changes in the workplace or website accessibility; it is about the type of accessibility work that will reshape ideologies and make social justice a central column in our field, a fundamental purpose of our work, and a spark to revolutionize the whole social system.

The Paradigm Shift

The paradigm shift is a call to include disabled users in our usability tests and to center disability in our practices and research. Coined by Marie Theofanos and Janice Redish (2005), this call recurred in the articles from the special issues (Bayer & Pappas, 2006; Brys & Vanderbauwhede, 2006; Sherena Huntsman et al., 2018; Lisa Melonçon, 2018; Nielsen, 2018; Roberts, 2006; Thea Van Der Geest, 2006), as well as in works outside of the special issues. The call for the paradigm shift appears everywhere in TPC scholarship on accessibility, but it has not materialized fully into actions. Indeed, this call first appeared in Jodie Gilmore’s 1989 article in TC (without being named “the paradigm shift”) and is still the primary focus in IEEE’s 2021 introduction, underscoring the long history in the field of this ignored call.

Disability as a Gain for TPC

This concept means that rather than framing disability as a problem to solve, it is perceived as an important addition that deepens the field’s teaching and workplace practices as well as its theoretical work. While this idea is implicit in earlier articles, we find one of its first explicit mentions in Annika Konrad’s (2018) article in BPCQ 81.1, which argued that thinking about disability can fuel innovative thinking in the workplace, pushing back against ingrained ableist ideologies. Similarly, Bennett and Hannah (2021) called attention to the important insights that disabled embodiments bring to transforming the workplace. Focusing on our courses, Sonka et al. (2021) argued that teaching about disability in our TPC classrooms can lead to deep conversations with students about social justice. From a praxis perspective,

  • Kevin Garrison (2018) illustrated how understanding the process of lip reading gives us insights on how to create better interface design and gain a deeper understanding of human communication;
  • Gonzales (2018) brought the interdependency concept from disability studies into TPC to illustrate how the sum of accessible and linguistically diverse translations is better than its separate parts; and
  • Matthew Baker et al. (2021) underscored how learning about adaptive technology makes us better TPCers.
