IEEE Transactions on Professional Communication (IEEE) 1985 Special Issue

Writing for the Disadvantaged Reader (28.4)



One of the earliest articles in TPC publications to focus on the topic of disability appeared in 1975 in BPCQ (before the journal had its present name). Actually, this text is not an article, but an authorless, one-page how-to presenting excerpts from job application letters from TPC students with disabilities (“Four Ways,” 1975). There had not been much else published on the topic until the first special issue on disability and accessibility, published in 1985 by IEEE.

I am being generous by describing the 1985 IEEE special issue as focused on disability and accessibility. In fact, it was focused on “disadvantaged readers” (Valerie Arms, p. 1), which included non-native English speakers and people with hearing impairments. The authors of the articles and the introduction portrayed people with disabilities in deficit terms, calling them “problem readers” (Andrew Malcolm, 1985b, p. 2) who had a “communicative handicap” (Gerard Walter, p. 21) and deviated from the norm of the nondisabled user. For example, Kathleen Eilers Crandall spent considerable time detailing the reasons Deaf people have reading difficulties due to “weaknesses in their knowledge of the semantics and syntax of English... gaps between the background knowledge they bring to the reading task and that assumed by the author… [and unfamiliarity] with discourse organization techniques” (p. 5). Through the negative language of disadvantage, both people with disability and non-native speakers were presented in a deficit model, where communicating through American Sign Language and speaking more than one language become less-than situations.

Through this deficit perspective on disability, the authors presented TPCers as saviors, rescuing people with disabilities from dark, isolated lives by creating closed captions (Dave Crane; Malcolm, 1985a) and other accommodations (Crandall 1985; Alinda Drury, 1985). TPCers and engineers are never represented as having disabilities themselves. The work they were doing in accessibility only helped the nonstandard group of “disadvantaged readers” (Arms, p. 1). Thus, accommodations were exclusively for the use of people with disabilities and serve no purpose for other users. Indeed, in the article by Crane, the Deaf were depicted as dragged out of their isolation from mainstream society through the simple addition of closed captions to allow them to watch television:

Not only does a real-time captioning enable hearing-impaired persons to benefit from the news, but it also allows this segment of the population to enjoy many live events which the general population take for granted today such as the launch of the space shuttles, the Academy Awards, the 1984 political conventions and debates, election night coverage, and the 1984 Summer Olympics. (p. 18)

In this well-meaning but ableist idea of the Deaf community, the Deaf only needed a saving grace from technology—closed captions—for not being “deaf” anymore, and there was neither a larger conversation on the systemic discrimination that they faced at every level of society nor on how captions actually help everyone.

In 1985, IEEE was a journal focusing mostly on the communication needs of engineers since it was created with the aim of improving the written communication of engineers (Procomm, 2025a). In the preface for this issue, the editor Valerie Arms stated that “professional communicators should have a better understanding of how difficult good communication is, as they consider the various perspectives offered in this special issue” (p. 1). All the authors in this issue identified their audience as engineers, and the articles’ collective purpose is to help engineers improve their writing skills. While Walter (1985) and Larry LoMaglio and Victoria Robinson (1985) had a methods-results structure, most of these articles were short and practice oriented, focusing on a writing issue and providing strategies for creating clearer texts. Most of the articles followed the structure of describing the problems of “disadvantaged readers,” followed by a solution to the problems that focuses on engineers paying attention to an aspect of their own writing. The articles called for engineers to write to “include” all users (LoMaglio & Robinson, p. 27) and to “not be content to apply a well-known readability formula” as their only effort towards readability (Drury, p. 14).

The impulse to create this special issue and help engineers improve their writing so that all readers can understand their texts showed a well-meaning intent to give the topic of disability (and non-native speakers) the spotlight (at least for one issue). Indeed, the journal editor, Valerie Arms, described the articles in the special issue as following “a common theme of increasing goodwill and understanding” (p. 1). Benevolence, then, was the impetus for accessibility work in 1985.

Of course, the 1985 issue was published before the passing of the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990) and before disability studies had established itself as a recognized field. Thus, the perspectives on accessibility in this early issue reflected that the field of TPC was following the stereotypically negative and simplistic ideas on disabilities shared by the rest of society at the time. In this issue, “disadvantaged readers” were outside the norm and therefore required extra attention. None of the articles offered a reflection on how society itself is disabling, which leads to framing the accessibility work by TPCers as a kind, benevolent, apolitical thing to do. TPCers were not asked to tackle systemic ableism but to be “kind engineers” and fix the problems of their “disadvantaged readers.”

After the 1985 special issue, there were two more articles on disability in IEEE (Andrew Malcolm, 1987; Theo Manschreck, 1986) that continued the deficit perspective. However, we then find an important shift in the field. In 1989, Technical Communication (TC) published an article that acted as a complete contrast to the negative perspective found in the 1985 IEEE special issue. Focusing on how to make technical presentations accessible for the visually impaired, Jodie Gilmore (1989) introduced two important themes that will reappear throughout the upcoming special issues on disability:

  • Accessibility helps everyone.
  • We need to involve disabled users in the creation of our projects to ensure we are meeting their accessibility needs.

This is the very beginning of what Mary Frances Theofanos and Janice (Ginny) Redish (2005) will coin as the “paradigm shift” (p. 17), which call for centering accessibility in TPC projects and research. Thus, in 1989, we find an important change from the medical model of disability toward a much more nuanced and positive perspective on this topic. Accessibility work in TPC starts to be understood as a practice that helps all users and finds its place as central strategy within TPC work.

The next special issue on disability and accessibility is published in TC in 2006. In between Gilmore’s (1989) article and the 2006 special issue, we find articles reinforcing the positive connection between disability, accessibility, and TPC—thus preparing the terrain for the articles of the 2006 issue.
